# Introduction to Legal Reasoning & Case Analysis

:: Legal reasoning is the disciplined process by which lawyers move from facts to conclusions. The most common framework for doing this is IRAC.

## 1.1 The IRAC Method

IRAC stands for Issue, Rule, Application, Conclusion. It is not a rigid formula but a habit of thought — a way of forcing yourself to separate what the law says from what the facts show.

### Issue

What legal question is the court being asked to answer? Frame it as a single sentence beginning with "Whether…" — for example, "Whether a manufacturer owes a duty of care to a consumer with whom he has no contract."

### Rule

Which rule of law governs? Cite the statute, case, or principle. State it precisely, not vaguely. "Donoghue v Stevenson established the neighbour principle" is not a rule; the rule is the sentence Lord Atkin actually wrote.

### Application

Apply the rule to the specific facts. This is where marks are won and lost — courts want to see the rule *work* on the facts, not just be stated.

### Conclusion

Answer the question posed in Issue. One or two sentences. No hedging.

!!! **Why IRAC matters.** The structure forces you to separate the rule from the facts. A weak answer often collapses these two into one blurry paragraph — and a lecturer cannot find the rule to credit it.

## 1.2 Reading a Case

When you read a case, do not read it like a story. Read it for four things: the facts, the issue, the holding, and the reasoning.

> "You must take reasonable care to avoid acts or omissions which you can reasonably foresee would be likely to injure your neighbour."
> — Lord Atkin, *Donoghue v Stevenson* [1932] AC 562 at 580

That sentence is the rule. Everything after it is application. Notice how cleanly the two can be separated once you know what to look for.
