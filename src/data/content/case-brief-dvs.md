# Case Brief: Donoghue v Stevenson [1932] AC 562

:: The foundational negligence case establishing that a manufacturer owes a duty of care to the ultimate consumer even without a direct contract.

## 1.1 Facts

Facts: On 26 August 1928, May Donoghue consumed part of a bottle of ginger beer bought for her by a friend at a café in Paisley. The bottle was opaque. After drinking, she poured out the remainder and a decomposing snail fell out.[^1] She fell ill and sued the manufacturer, David Stevenson, for negligence.

## 1.2 Issue

Issue: Whether a manufacturer of a product owes a duty of care to a consumer with whom he has no direct contractual relationship.

## 1.3 Holding

Holding: Yes. A manufacturer owes a duty of care to the ultimate consumer to take reasonable care that the product is free from defects likely to cause injury.

## 1.4 Ratio

Ratio: The ==neighbour principle== articulated by Lord Atkin:

> "You must take reasonable care to avoid acts or omissions which you can reasonably foresee would be likely to injure your neighbour."
> — Lord Atkin, at 580

!!! The neighbour principle is **the** foundational rule of negligence. Every subsequent duty-of-care question is a refinement of this test, not a replacement.

## 1.5 Case comparison

| Case | Year | Contribution |
| --- | --- | --- |
| *Heaven v Pender* | 1883 | Precursor — duty owed in specific circumstances |
| *Donoghue v Stevenson* | 1932 | Established general neighbour principle |
| *Anns v Merton LBC* | 1978 | Expanded duty test (later overruled) |
| *Caparo v Dickman* | 1990 | Three-stage test replacing Anns |

## 1.6 Procedural history

--- p.562 ---

1. Court of Session (Outer House) — dismissed, no duty in absence of contract
2. Court of Session (Inner House) — affirmed
3. House of Lords — reversed; duty owed

--- p.580 ---

## 1.7 Notes for exams

- Always state the neighbour principle precisely, not approximately.
- Distinguish *ratio* from *obiter* — the neighbour principle is the ratio; observations on privity are the *obiter*.
- Remember that Donoghue was a Scottish case — but its rule is the foundation of Nigerian negligence law via reception.
